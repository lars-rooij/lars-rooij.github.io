Illegibility

Art is often discussed in terms of the relationship between representation and abstraction. However, how a work represents something, does not necessarily determine how easily it can be understood. A realistic image can remain ambiguous, while an abstract composition can communicate a clear idea. Recognition and comprehension are not the same thing. As a creative strategy, I propose Illegibility, the deliberate disruption of the relationship between a recognizable source and its interpretation. Through processes like distortion, fragmentation, repetition, subtraction, and recombination, artists can preserve traces of a source while making its identity, structure, or meaning difficult to access. Illegibility is not to be understood as being about the absence of meaning, but as a method that extrapolates and highlights the unfamiliar, alien, and strange, within things we usually recognize. In everyday communication, recognition is often treated as the first step towards understanding: a word is read, an object is identified, or a sound is interpreted. Illegibility disrupts this process, preserving enough of a source to activate recognition, but denying the information necessary for a complete reading. The viewer is consequently placed between familiarity and uncertainty, recognizing that something is present without being able to immediately determine what it means. As an artistic strategy, Illegibility shifts attention away from the immediate communication of information, and towards the processes through which information is organized, interpreted, and understood. The following five works demonstrate how I see this strategy present itself in different creative and performing arts.

In Writing

Xu Bing's Book from the Sky is a mixed-media installation comprising hand-printed books and hanging scrolls made using thousands of invented Chinese characters. Produced over four years, the work uses traditional printing techniques, and character forms inspired by historical Chinese typography. Although the characters appear authentic, none of them can be read as meaningful. Nonetheless, as visible in Figure 1, the carefully aligned rows, repeated patterns, and overall presentation of the printed pages aim to replicate the appearance of scholarly literature. The installation draws on the cultural significance and authority of books and writing, while deliberately withholding the knowledge that these forms tend to communicate.

Illegibility showcases itself in Xu Bing's Book from the Sky in its most literal sense: the written characters cannot be read, despite their convincing appearance. The artist deliberately creates thousands of meaningless characters, with the fact that no one can actually read them being central to the installation. As Xu Bing describes, “The sense of the sublime arises from the deliberate effort to reach a meaningless goal.”1 The work therefore does not just present illegible writing, but emphatically mimics a situation in which the viewer expects explicit meaning to be present.

Despite its illegibility, the installation still reproduces the structures through which written language acquires authority. The utilized traditional printing techniques, carefully ordered pages, and resemblance to classical literature, all communicate scholarly levels of cultural significance. At a glance, the viewer recognizes the work as writing and understands its purpose, but they cannot access any of the information that should be there. It is this contradiction, the appearance of legibility and the impossibility of reading, that is central to the work. Rather than abandoning representation, Xu Bing preserves its outward form while dismantling its communicative function. Book from the Sky thus demonstrates how Illegibility can emerge from a recognizable system, through the deliberate separation of writing's appearance from the meaning it is expected to convey.

In Logo Design

Xavlegbmaofffassssitimiwoamndutroabcwapwaeiippohfffx, commonly shortened to Xavleg, is a band that makes extreme metal music. Like the band's full name, its logo is visually dense. It is shown in Figure 2, made by Sergey Polyakov. Together, the name and logo give the band a highly stylized typographic identity. Unlike conventional logos, which usually prioritize immediate readability, extreme metal logos frequently employ these intricate letterings, compressed forms, sharp edges, and interwoven strokes. The characteristics allow typography to function as an expressive graphic image, alongside it being a means of identification.

This approach to logo design reflects a broader tendency within extreme metal, where they frequently deliberately obscure their lettering to the point of near-illegibility. Xavleg takes this convention to an extreme, extending the sheer audacity of its unusually long name into its identity. The band's own website describes the logo as unreadable, showing that this difficulty is an intentional feature, rather than a consequence of its design.2 The logo thus deliberately misconstrues the conventional purpose of typography.

Nevertheless, when looked at through the eyes of someone with the experience and skill necessary, the design still does contain recognizable letters corresponding to the band's actual name. An attempt at reconstruction of the “Deciphered” Xavleg Logo by Octave Industries aimed to identify these characters. This reconstruction demonstrates that a structure does exist beneath the apparent visual chaos. Nonetheless, through their deliberate extreme distortions and entanglement, these characters become difficult, if not impossible, to distinguish. Illegibility consequently becomes both the defining visual characteristic of the logo, and an exaggeration of the standards of the metal scene from which it emerged. The work demonstrates how a recognizable source can be transformed into an almost abstract image while retaining its original function as a distinctive band identity.

In Sculptures

Internal Object in Three Parts, by Anish Kapoor, explores the relationship between material, bodily associations, and abstract form. Kapoor's wider sculptural practice often explores perception, spatial ambiguity, and the relationship between an object's physical presence and the viewer's interpretation of it. In Figure 3, the work's three-part arrangement manifests itself as alien, fleshy surfaces consisting of deep reds, pale whites, folds, lumps, and more. These features give the impression of biological tissue, without resolving a clear anatomical structure. They create uncertainty about whether the forms represent something recognizable, or exist as independent sculptural objects.

Despite the internal object clearly mimicking bodily interiors, its individual parts are difficult to identify. The objects are detached from the usual contexts that signify something as a fleshy anatomical structure. They obscure the features that might allow the viewer to determine specific organs, tissues, or bones. Kapoor has created a vague mess of flesh-like material, rather than a clear anatomical representation. The viewer recognizes the suggestions of meat, bones, and internal matter. But they can struggle to identify distinct structures, or their underlying relationships.

This transformation demonstrates how Illegibility can emerge through the alteration of a recognizable physical source. The work retains its association with the interior of the body, yet its scale, irregularity, and lack of anatomical clarity, sabotage the possibility of a clear reading. Lying between representation and abstraction, the source remains perceptible, but its defining characteristics have been obscured. Kapoor seems to embrace the reference to bodily material, transforming it until nothing else is left. Its Illegibility arises through the contrast between knowing what kind of subject the work evokes, and being unable to determine exactly what its forms represent.

In Sound Art

Alvin Lucier's I Am Sitting in a Room is a sound work first realized in 1969, in which the artist records his own voice speaking a short text, then repeatedly plays back and re-records the recording in the same room. An exemplar of a full recording can be found here. With each iteration, the newly recorded version replaces the previous one, slowly changing the characteristics of those first spoken words. The room’s acoustic properties enhance certain frequencies, while weakening others, causing the speech to become increasingly distorted over iterations. While the recording is initially a clear and easily understandable voice, the repeated transformations obscure the words until they become more like the resonant tones of the room itself. The work shifts from recognizable speech, towards an increasingly abstract composition.

The transformation in I Am Sitting in a Room comes from the process of repetition with small alterations, rather than deliberate manipulation of the original recording. Each iteration preserves the previous version while introducing further changes, accumulating the room’s resonant frequencies over time. As the process continues, the original content becomes less distinguishable, while the other acoustic increase their presence. Lucier’s voice remains the source of the sound, yet its original communicative function largely disappears. Thus, the work uses repetition and its minute changes, until those changes are the only legible thing that remain.

This process exemplifies Illegibility, through its slow descent of intelligible language into its absence. The work highlights how the physical properties of the recording environment transform the original iteration. The room functions not simply as a container for the voice, but as an active transformation applied to the work. Although the original text establishes a recognizable linguistic structure, the repetition is what eventually renders that entirely inaccessible, divorced and unrecognizable from its source. I Am Sitting in a Room therefore demonstrates how Illegibility can emerge through the repeated transformation of a familiar source, in which meaning gradually gives way to the material conditions which exhibit its transmission.

In Videography

Mona Hatoum's Corps Étranger is a video installation created using endoscopic imagery of the artist's own body. The work combines moving images and bodily sounds within a cylindrical installation, where footage is projected onto a circular screen at floor level. A section of the video displayed can be found here. In the video, the camera travels across the body's exterior, before entering its internal pathways. It presents a view of surfaces and cavities that are not ordinarily available for direct observation. As illustrated by some screenshots of the video in Figure 4, the endoscopic camera removes the familiarity of our body, which we usually only know from the outside. The result transforms the human body into an unfamiliar and strange visual experience.

The title, which translates as “foreign body,” reinforces this central intention of the work: the internal structures that make up our bodies, appear alien when encountered through this type of visual access. The video begins with the outside of the artist's face, establishing a recognizable subject before steadily moving into images that turn into something difficult to interpret without context. This transition, from the familiar exterior to the unfamiliar interior, can create a shift in the viewer's perception. It transformes something ordinarily understood as part of the self, into something strange and extremely foreign.

Despite this visual alienation, the installation makes it clear that the images originate from the human body. The source remains identifiable, even as its individual structures become unrecognizable. The transformation does not hide that this is a body, but makes the familiar appear unfamiliar. The focus on internal elements, the removal of other contextual cues, and the immersive presentation, disrupt the viewer's ability to recognize the body as it normally would. Hatoum thus demonstrates how Illegibility can emerge through a transformation of perspective, in which a recognizable source is rendered alien without losing its underlying identity.

Thus,

I propose Illegibility as a creative strategy, in which recognizable sources are transformed until their identities, structures, or meanings become difficult to access. Across writing, graphic design, sculpture, sound, and video installation, the strategy demonstrates that recognition is not a fixed property of an object. It is more a process, shaped by the context, perception, and expectation an observer has. Xu Bing disrupts the authority of written language, Xavleg transforms iconography into an indecipherable visual form, Kapoor complicates the recognition of bodily material, Lucier allows repetition to erode linguistic meaning, and Hatoum makes the familiar interior of the body appear foreign.

Despite their differences, these works retain traces of the sources from which they emerge. Illegibility creates something which remains recognizable to an extent, but that recognition is no longer sufficient to produce a stable interpretation. As a method of artistic creation, Illegibility can emerge through distortion, fragmentation, repetition, subtraction, recombination, or changes in perspective. Its significance lies in its ability to make uncertainty, unrecognizability, and inscrutability themselves central to a work. Rather than treating immediate comprehension as the ultimate goal of art, Illegibility allows artists to investigate the limits of recognition. It can create works whose meanings only emerge through prolonged attention, ambiguity, and personal interpretations.
